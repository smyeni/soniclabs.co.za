Digital Signal Processing

The DFT

You don't necessarily need to memorize every DSP derivation to develop strong intuition. If you can see phasors, understand complex multiplication as rotation and scaling, and understand vector addition as interference, a surprisingly large part of DSP becomes tangible.

The Sonic Way

Start with the geometry

Before diving into the DFT equation, it helps to understand what the mathematics is actually doing.

Phasors

A complex number can be viewed as a rotating vector in the complex plane. Its length represents magnitude and its angle represents phase.

Multiplication

Complex multiplication combines scaling with rotation. Multiplying by a unit phasor rotates a vector through a particular angle.

Addition

Adding phasors is vector addition. Depending on their relative phases, they reinforce one another or cancel.

The key intuition: the DFT repeatedly rotates and adds phasors, asking how strongly the sampled signal aligns with a particular frequency.
The Mathematics

The Discrete Fourier Transform

Given a sequence of N samples, the DFT calculates how strongly the signal corresponds to each of N discrete frequency bins.

X[k] = ∑ x[n] e−j2πkn/N

The exponential term is a rotating phasor. For each frequency bin, the DFT applies a different phase-advance rate to the samples and sums the resulting vectors.

Each bin is a phase chaser

Think of every DFT bin as a chaser travelling around the unit circle at its own particular phase-advance rate.

When the chaser's rotation matches a frequency present in the signal, the resulting phasors tend to point in a common direction. They reinforce one another and produce a large magnitude.

When the rotation does not match, the phasors tend to spread around the circle and cancel one another.

That is the DFT in geometric terms: rotate, add, and observe whether the vectors reinforce or cancel.
